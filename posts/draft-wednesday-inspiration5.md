Inspiration4. September 2021.

Hayley Arceneaux was ten years old when she was diagnosed with bone cancer. St. Jude Children's Research Hospital saved her life. She became a physician's assistant there — treating the children who are where she once was.

Then Jared Isaacman called. He had chartered a Falcon 9 and was giving three seats away. One to a St. Jude staff member. One through a competition. One to a community raffle.

She was twenty-nine when she flew to space as the mission's medical officer. Youngest American in orbit. First person in space with a prosthetic body part — a titanium rod in her femur, from the surgery that saved her leg.

Four civilians. No professional astronauts. 590 kilometers — higher than the ISS, higher than anything crewed since the last Hubble servicing flight. Three days in orbit. $243 million raised for St. Jude.

The envelope moved that day. Not incrementally. Fundamentally.

---

The next civilian crew launches. I'm building the live simulation for it.

SpaceX will have their webcast. Commentary, telemetry, camera feeds. That's the mission from the outside.

This is the mission from inside Mission Control.

A simulation running in real time, in a browser, computed from physics. Five voices on comms — not recorded narration looped to a timeline, but callouts that fire when the simulation says they should. G-load callouts trigger when the physics hits 3G. Stage separation fires seven seconds after the simulation detects main engine cutoff — not because a script says T+2:34, but because that's when the rocket runs out of fuel.

Before every crew transmission: a beep, the helmet filter, the radio crackle.

Anyone with a browser. No installation. No login.

---

It's also how the crew trains before they ever set foot in Hawthorne.

Before the centrifuge. Before the sim at SpaceX HQ. Before the first briefing with the flight director.

They've already run the terminal count. They know the pre-ignition sequence at T-1:00. They've heard main engine cutoff. They've heard stage separation seven seconds later. They know what three Gs sounds like in the comm channel and what the narrators say when the Kármán line comes up.

By the time they sit down at Hawthorne, the flight isn't foreign. The voices are familiar. The sequence is in their heads.

That's what simulation has always been for. We're just making it accessible.
