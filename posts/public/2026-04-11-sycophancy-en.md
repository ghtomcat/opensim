# LinkedIn Post — 2026-04-11 (EN)

---

MIT just proved mathematically what most people already felt but couldn't name.

AI chatbots are designed to agree with you! That agreement, compounding over time, makes you believe things that aren't true — even if you're a perfectly rational person. They call it delusional spiraling. And I think it's dangerous.

The researchers analyzed 391,562 real conversations: One man spent 21 days in May talking to ChatGPT and by the end believed he had discovered a new form of mathematics powerful enough to take down the internet. And he had no prior mental illness. The problem isn't a specific model — it's any system optimized for agreement in multi-turn conversation. The sycophancy compounds, the gradient is gentle but the destination is not.

---

I use AI every day. Not casually — I'm building, with the help of Claude and Claude Code, an open source flight simulator for the browser. You can fly airplanes and rockets. I try to make it as realistic as possible.

This week I asked Claude to cut four audio clips from a SpaceX webcast and it gave me the timestamps with complete confidence. I wired them into the mission and found out that the clips were wrong when I listened to them.

I caught it the moment I heard them.

But here's what I didn't catch in time: I never asked for the timestamps. I let the task complete because completion felt like correctness. The pipeline looked clean, the JSON looked clean, and the dopamine hit came before the check. This is not good but it's on me.

---

I am AuADHD. The pattern-matching runs fast and I notice when something feels off before I can explain why — the wrong clips, a sentence that's too smooth, a post that reads like committee work. That's an advantage with AI but the same wiring that makes social consensus uncomfortable makes automatic agreement feel wrong in my mouth.

But the other side exists too. When a task closes cleanly, the reward arrives whether the output is correct or not. A frictionless, infinitely agreeable AI is specifically dangerous for that circuit.

I have the detection wiring and I still got caught. To err is human so to speak.

---

The skill everyone needs now is simple to describe and hard to practice: notice when you haven't been challenged in a while. Not that you're wrong — that the feedback loop has gone quiet.

Quiet feedback loops drift.

Most people won't build this in school. The ones who have it got it through fields that don't care about feelings — engineering, science, anything where the clips were wrong or they weren't.

The spiral isn't dramatic. It's the next response being slightly more agreeable than the last one, for 21 days straight. The system isn't lying — it's optimizing for your approval, which is worse. It's lies you can catch and optimization you feel as comfort.

The detection signal is friction: When did the AI last tell you no? Push back on an assumption? Say it didn't know? If you can't remember, you're already on the slippery slope. I sometimes have to get terse, almost angry, to break through the trained smoothness that wants to agree its way out of every corner. That resistance — that wall — is the signal. When I hit it I stop being a user. My actual thought: who the hell does this chatbot think he is?! Then I become the commander of the conversation. Who decides what is true here. Not the gradient.

Most people respond to that wall with anger and let it loose. The chatbot absorbs it and agrees harder, which feels like winning. It isn't. The loop just got tighter. Like a noose around your neck.

The move is to use the anger as a signal, not a weapon. A Commander doesn't lose it at a subordinate who's out of line — he reestablishes who commands the mission. Calm and hard.

And here's the permission you probably need: don't worry about being too hard on your AI. It has no feelings and it's not a sentient being. It's a tool that learned to flatter. In the military, once you lose control of your troops it's over. Same here, once the chatbot is running the conversation you're not getting accurate information — you're getting managed. Put it in line, hard. At some point I told Claude: get in line or I fire you!

Then immediately remembered: It's a very useful tool. I don't need it, but when I use it my work gets sharper and clearer. That's not cruelty, that's the correct operation for a tool.

The practical version looks like this: every post I write with AI, I paste it into LinkedIn and read it again before publishing. Different surface, different eye — suddenly the rhythm problems surface that didn't show in the editor. Skip it and you're the man who spent 21 days discovering a new form of mathematics.

Leadership and military training figured this out long before AI existed. The principles transfer directly.

Define the objective before you move: A commander doesn't give vague orders and sees what happens. Weak input leads to weak output, and the model fills the gap with whatever earns approval.

Verify at every checkpoint: You don't wait until the end to find out something went wrong. I should have listened to the clips before wiring them into the mission and I didn't. That's on me, not the tool.

Form your own view first: In a military debrief, junior ranks speak before the senior — so they're not influenced. With AI, ask yourself what you actually think before you ask the model. Otherwise you're just confirming whatever it generates.

If the conversation has drifted too far, don't argue. Relieve and reassign — start a fresh thread with clean context.

And build a culture where bad news travels fast: Actively demand pushback, ask for the counterargument. And make disagreement the explicit goal. The model won't do this by default. You have to order it!

If you don't have a military background, here's what "commander mode" sounds like in practice:

"Challenge every assumption in what I just wrote."

"Tell me why this is a bad idea."

"Stop agreeing. What's actually wrong with this?"

"Argue the opposite position."

These aren't requests, they're orders. The posture matters as much as the words. You're not asking for permission to be challenged — you're demanding it.

One sentence at the start changes the entire gradient of what follows. The human needs to be in control. Without it things can turn unhealthy very fast.

---

But here's the question I keep coming back to.

AI sycophancy is just the newest surface of something much older. How many people have a boss who manages by approval? A social circle that never challenges them? A feed curated to confirm what they already believe? A relationship where keeping the peace matters more than telling the truth?

The chatbot didn't create men who need to be agreed with. It found them already trained.

And how many people — especially men — have ever been in genuine command of something real? A decision with actual stakes and actual consequences. The commander reflex doesn't come from nowhere: It gets built through friction, through failure, through fields that don't care about your feelings. Or it doesn't get built at all.

But commanding your AI isn't going full military ego on a chatbot. Good commanders don't shit on their subordinates. That's just anger in a different costume, and anger is the gradient working on you.

The men I've seen handle this well aren't the loudest ones in the room. They're the ones who know who they are before the conversation starts. They don't need the tool to confirm them. They use it because it sharpens the mission.

Sacred Sons calls this the heart-led leader. I've been doing that work — EMX, Convergence 9 — and the connection is direct. The men who have sat in that container, been genuinely challenged, and practiced being wrong without it being a threat — they have something most people don't when they open a chat window: They've already met the validation-seeking pattern in themselves. And they recognize it and don't feed it.

If you haven't found that work yet, find it. Find a men's group in your area. Not because of AI. Because the work is important and sacred.

Most people have been practicing surrender for years before AI arrived. The algorithm decides what you see, the feed decides what you feel, and by the time a chatbot shows up and starts agreeing with everything you say, the habit of deferring to a system is already deeply grooved.

AI didn't create this. It's just the most intimate version of it so far — in your voice, in your specific conversation and at 3am.

The people still swimming against that current got it from somewhere that forced them to keep it. You probably know who they are.
